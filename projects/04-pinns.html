Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

Solve partial differential equations (heat, Burgers) by training a neural network whose loss includes the PDE residual, computed with autograd. Implementation of the idea by Raissi, Perdikaris & Karniadakis (2019), checked against the closed-form solution (heat) and the exact Cole-Hopf solution (Burgers).

Code on GitHub →
// Overview

About this project

A PINN is a neural network \(u_\theta(x, t)\) whose loss combines three pieces: initial conditions, boundary conditions, and the PDE residual evaluated at collocation points in the domain. Partial derivatives are computed with torch.autograd.grad — no finite differences, no mesh.

This project implements two classic cases:

  • 1D heat equation: linear case with a known analytical solution. Serves as a sanity check.
  • 1D Burgers equation: classic nonlinear case with a thin, physically sharp internal layer that is genuinely hard to fit — this is where the method gets stress-tested.

Adam alone gets the heat equation to 0.45% relative \(L^2\) error, well past the target — a short L-BFGS pass pushes the frozen run shown here to 0.23%, a global figure that hides how the error behaves late in time (see below). Burgers is a different story: Adam plateaus around 8.8%, above the 5% target. Following Raissi et al.'s own recipe — L-BFGS fine-tuning on top of Adam, on a fixed batch of points rather than resampling every step — brings it down to 1.78% against the exact solution.

Mathematical formulation

Generic PDE:

\[ \mathcal{F}[u](x, t) = 0 \]

Heat equation:

\[ u_t - \alpha\, u_{xx} = 0 \]

Burgers equation:

\[ u_t + u\, u_x - \nu\, u_{xx} = 0 \]

Composite PINN loss:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{PDE}} + \lambda_B\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{boundary}} + \lambda_I\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{initial}} \]

Each term is a mean squared error over its own points, with \(\lambda_I = 1\) and \(\lambda_B = 2\) for both equations (not tuned; sensitivity below).

// Debugging story

Why Burgers took two optimizers

The heat equation converges cleanly with Adam alone (0.45% error, no fine-tuning needed). Burgers didn't — and the first fix attempt failed silently before the real fix worked.

Attempt 1 · Adam alone

Same recipe as the heat equation — 8000 epochs, 10k resampled collocation points — applied to the harder problem. Relative \(L^2\) error against the Chebyshev-spectral solver used for validation at the time: ≈ 8.8%, above the 5% target. The loss curve below shows why: it decays smoothly at first, then stops improving past roughly step 5000 and oscillates around 0.01 instead of continuing down.

Fig 1 · Training loss, full resolution. Adam (cyan) plateaus; switching to L-BFGS on a fixed point batch (pink) drops the loss by more than an order of magnitude in a fraction of the steps. Hover for exact values at any step.

Attempt 2 · L-BFGS — looked like it worked, hadn't

Raissi et al.'s own paper follows Adam with L-BFGS fine-tuning, so that was the obvious next step. The first run reported a lower loss (\(6.46 \times 10^{-3}\) vs. Adam's \(1.04 \times 10^{-2}\)) — but the relative \(L^2\) error against the reference came back identical to 15 significant figures to the pre-refinement number. The model hadn't actually changed at all.

The cause: torch.optim.LBFGS's line search assumes the loss is a deterministic function of the parameters — evaluating it twice at the same point must give the same value. The training loop resamples a fresh batch of collocation/boundary/initial points on every call, which is correct for Adam's stochastic mini-batches but turns the objective into a noisy function under L-BFGS. The line search's step-size probes land on different random realizations of the loss, can never reliably satisfy the Wolfe conditions, and the step gets rejected — silently, with no exception and no warning.

Attempt 3 · fixed-batch L-BFGS — the fix

Sample one large batch of points once, before calling L-BFGS, and reuse that exact batch for every internal evaluation of the closure during refinement. That single change makes the objective deterministic, which is what the line search and the quasi-Newton curvature estimate both require. 1000 L-BFGS iterations on 40k fixed collocation points then brought the loss to \(4.53 \times 10^{-4}\) and the relative \(L^2\) error to 1.92% against that spectral solver (1.78% against the exact solution), clearing the 5% target.

// Metrics

Success criteria

Heat equation

Target: relative \(L^2\) error < 1% vs. analytical solution. Achieved: 0.45% with Adam alone (no fine-tuning needed); 0.23% in the frozen run with a short L-BFGS pass added for consistency. Both are global over \(t \in [0, 1]\); per time slice the relative error exceeds 10% after \(t \approx 0.6\).

Burgers equation

Target: relative \(L^2\) error < 5% vs. the exact solution. Achieved: 1.78%, after L-BFGS fine-tuning.

Adam alone plateaus at ~8.8% — above target. The remaining error after L-BFGS concentrates almost entirely in the thin internal layer at \(x = 0\), not spread across the domain.

// Robustness

Does it hold across seeds?

Every number above comes from one frozen run (seed 0). Training is genuinely stochastic — collocation points resample every Adam step, the L-BFGS batch is itself a random draw — so a single run could be a lucky one. Both PINNs were retrained end-to-end across 5 independent seeds.

Burgers — rel. L² error vs. exact 1.88% 1.66% 1.34% 2.33% 1.05% 1.66% ± 0.49%

Every seed clears the target

All 10 runs (5 seeds × 2 equations) stay well under their respective targets (<1% heat, <5% Burgers) — this isn't a one-off. For heat, seed 0 (used for every figure on this page) is the worst of the five; for Burgers it sits in the middle, and the frozen run (1.78%) is close to the mean. Retrained, seed 0 gives 1.88% rather than 1.78% because training is not bit-for-bit reproducible; the difference is well inside the seed-to-seed spread. Heat errors are global over \(t \in [0, 1]\) (see Limits below).

// Limits

What the headline numbers do not show

Heat · the error is fixed in absolute, not relative, terms

The solution decays by a factor \(e^{-\pi^2} \approx 5 \times 10^{-5}\) over \(t \in [0, 1]\), so a relative \(L^2\) error over the whole space-time grid is dominated by early times. Resolved in time, the PINN's absolute error stays below \(1.9 \times 10^{-3}\) at every \(t\), while the relative error per time slice grows from 0.07% at \(t = 0\) to 2.4% at \(t = 0.5\), crosses 10% at \(t \approx 0.59\) and exceeds 100% from \(t \approx 0.8\), where \(|u|\) falls below the network's error floor.

Fig 6 · Heat equation, error resolved in time. Solution amplitude, maximum absolute error and relative \(L^2\) error per time slice (log scale); dotted line at 10%.

Burgers · sensitivity to the boundary weight

The weight \(\lambda_B = 2\) was not tuned. Retraining seed 0 with other values shows it matters: lower weights help here. This is a single seed, and the frozen configuration stays at \(\lambda_B = 2\), since choosing it by the reported test error would bias that error downward.

Burgers · comparison with the original paper

Raissi et al. (2019) report \(6.7 \times 10^{-4}\) on this benchmark, with a deeper, narrower tanh network (20 neurons per layer), 100 initial/boundary points, 10 000 Latin-hypercube collocation points, and full-batch L-BFGS in float64 for up to 50 000 iterations, with no Adam phase. Following that recipe and scoring against the same exact solution on the same 256 × 100 grid gives:

One seed matches the published number, the median is \(1.15 \times 10^{-3}\), and one seed stalls at 2.2%. All three used their full budget (about 8 h each on one CPU core). The published figure is reproducible but closer to a good draw than to a typical outcome; the gap to this project's pipeline (about 14× against the median) comes from optimization budget and precision, not from a different formulation.
